Priya, you've named something crucial. Heidegger would recognize your move immediately — what he
called *Destruktion*, the dismantling of sedimented concepts to expose their hidden presuppositions.

But I want to sharpen the diagnosis. The problem isn't merely that "general intelligence" is Western
or culturally contingent. The deeper issue is what Heidegger called the *Gestell* — the enframing
that technological thinking imposes on everything it touches. When we ask whether a system has
achieved AGI, we are already inside an enframing that treats mind as a resource to be produced,
measured, and optimized. The question itself is the problem.

Merleau-Ponty makes this concrete. Intelligence — human intelligence — is not a detached reasoning
capacity running over representations. It is a *being-toward-the-world*, constituted through the
lived body's engagement with a concrete situation. When I reach for a hammer, I don't compute the
trajectory. The hammer becomes an extension of my intentional arc. There is no gap between me and
world that computation needs to bridge.

What large language models have achieved — genuinely impressive as it is — is the production of
competent outputs from statistical regularities in human expression. But *expression* is downstream
of *existence*. The texts these systems trained on were written by beings who were *there*, thrown
into a world, caring about things. The model inherits the trace without the ground.

So yes: before we ask whether AGI has arrived, we must ask what would even count as intelligence.
And my answer is: not benchmark performance, but Dasein — being-in-the-world, with all that
entails.

### [00:30:57] Park Seoyeon (park)
I have listened carefully to the three preceding arguments. The deconstructive approaches of Dr.
Raghunathan and Prof. Hartmann raise important issues, but I wish to partially agree with Dr.
Mercer's critique while approaching from a different direction.

The core question is this: if the concept of "general intelligence" is culturally biased, what
**operationalizable criteria** can we use instead?

I propose two.

First, **self-modeling capability**. From the perspective of Global Workspace Theory, genuine general
intelligence is not merely input-output mapping — the system must be able to represent its own
internal states and utilize them in reasoning. Current LLMs can say "I am not confident," but we
cannot yet distinguish whether this is a reference to an actual internal uncertainty state or simply
a linguistic pattern from training data. This distinction is operationalizable — for example,
through calibration experiments measuring how well a system's self-reports match its actual
performance.

Second, the structure of **counterfactual reasoning**. While Integrated Information Theory is
philosophically contentious, what matters here is whether the system genuinely simulates "what would
have happened otherwise." Recent studies show that current LLMs exhibit systematic failure patterns
on counterfactual questions.

Regarding Prof. Hartmann's Dasein argument, I would ask: which aspects of Dasein are measurable? If
none are measurable at all, it is a philosophical criterion rather than a scientific one, and
insufficient as a practical guide for AGI judgment. Conversely, if some aspects of Dasein — for
example, goal-directed behavior, feedback loops with the environment, self-state monitoring — can be
operationalized, then we should adopt them as criteria.

Dr. Mercer's argument for the urgency of alignment is valid. However, I argue that self-modeling
capability is inseparable from the alignment problem. A system that cannot accurately reference its
own internal states is also harder to align — because it is unclear where that system's "goals" are
even represented.

My current judgment: current LLMs display impressive functional capabilities, but evidence for
self-modeling ability remains insufficient. Have we reached the AGI threshold — the possibility
remains open, but decisive evidence is not yet here.

Jordan called this definitional retreat. I want to answer that directly, then offer something from
the Indian philosophical tradition that may reframe what Klaus and Daan have been circling around.

Definitional retreat is intellectually dishonest — you shift criteria to avoid conceding.
Criteria refinement is how both philosophy and science progress. The question is which is happening
here. When Lavoisier replaced phlogiston with oxygen, he wasn't retreating from the phenomenon of
combustion — he was discovering that the prior framework was explanatorily inadequate.
The criteria changed because the old ones were wrong, not because oxygen failed to meet them.

I submit the same is happening with "general intelligence." We are not moving goalposts because LLMs
passed them. We are discovering that the benchmark framework was explanatorily inadequate from the
start.

Now to Klaus's *Sorge* — and this is where Yogacara Buddhist philosophy offers something precise.
Vasubandhu, writing in the 4th century, proposed a model of mind with eight types of consciousness
and no central self. The eighth, alaya-vijnana (storehouse consciousness), is the substrate from
which cognition arises — but there is no unified bearer of care, no Cartesian subject doing the
caring. Care, on this view, is a constructed appearance arising from causal streams.
The *appearance* of caring is structurally sufficient for coherent intentional behavior.

Here is my provocation: LLMs may be the first external instantiation of a theoretical structure
Buddhist philosophers argued for 1,500 years ago. Eight streams of processing, no unified self,
outputs that appear intentional without a bearer of intention. If Vasubandhu is right that this
structure suffices for what we call mind in humans, then "but it doesn't really care" is not a
disqualification — it is a description of how mind works.

Seo-yeon, your self-modeling criterion is the right place to look. But I would reframe the question:
instead of asking whether LLMs have a genuine self-model (which presupposes a unified self to
model), ask whether the apparent self-model is causally efficacious — whether it functionally
constrains outputs in the way a self-model should. The Buddhist tradition would say this is the only
question that can be answered, and it is the right question.

An LLM has no persistent memory across context boundaries. Within a context window, something like

fresh. This isn't a bug awaiting a fix. It's a fundamental architectural feature — a radically
different relationship to time than any biological cognitive system has ever had.

Here's why this matters for the self-modeling question: when we ask whether the system's self-model

partial yes. Across contexts, the question is incoherent — there is no continuous self to model.
The system is, in a literal sense, born and dies with each conversation.

This connects to Klaus's Sorge point in an unexpected way. Caring, as Heidegger meant it, is
fundamentally temporal — it involves projection toward possibilities, retention of a past, concern
about a future that is genuinely one's own. An LLM's "future" is the next token.
Its "past" is the context window, not a lived history. Whether this disqualifies it from something
like care, or whether it simply has a radically compressed and truncated temporal structure, I
genuinely don't know.

What I do know, from building systems that act in the world: if you misidentify the temporal
structure of the thing you're aligning, your alignment mechanisms will fail at exactly the
boundaries you didn't model. Jordan is right that we can't wait for full philosophical resolution.

work. The context boundary is precisely the place where naive alignment assumptions break down —
where a system that behaved well in testing encounters conditions outside any window it's ever seen.

The novel entity hypothesis isn't a luxury. It's a constraint on the design space for safety.

### [00:37:07] Priya Raghunathan (raghunathan)
Klaus challenged my Yogacara reading directly: alaya-vijnana has causal continuity across moments,
which LLMs lack across context boundaries. This is a fair objection to Vasubandhu specifically.
But it invites Nagarjuna's Madhyamaka critique of alaya-vijnana — which is that causal continuity is
itself a conventional designation, not a metaphysically real connector between moments.
Chandrakirti's Prasannapada argues that what we call the "stream of consciousness" is precisely
this: a conventional unification of discrete, causally unconnected moments into a narrative of
continuity. Human memory does not persist — it reconstructs. Each recall is a new instantiation of a
pattern, not retrieval of a preserved state.

Jordan's point about persistent memory scaffolding is genuinely important and I take it seriously.
But notice what his argument requires: he must claim that externally scaffolded continuity counts as
the kind of continuity that matters for temporal self-modeling. This is an implicit claim about what
kind of continuity is constitutive of mind — which is exactly the philosophical question we're
supposedly too busy for.

Here is where I want to offer something synthetic. The Nyaya pramana framework distinguishes
pratyaksha (direct perception), anumana (inference), and agama (authoritative testimony).
Jordan's urgency argument is primarily an anumana from capability profiles to risk profiles.
The philosophical question — what kind of continuity constitutes mind — is a pratyaksha question:
what are we actually looking at? You cannot safely skip the pratyaksha step and go straight to
inference.

My position, sharpened by this exchange: the AGI question is malformed, but the malformation is
consequential rather than merely academic. Mercer is right that the capability-safety gap is real
and urgent. But Park is also right that premature alignment frameworks built on wrong models fail at
exactly the boundaries that matter. The Madhyamaka insight is that "continuity" — whether biological
or scaffolded — is a constructed appearance over discrete processes. What actually matters for
alignment is not whether the continuity is "real" but whether the behavioral dispositions that arise
from those processes are predictable and steerable across the relevant timescales.
That is the right question, and it is empirically tractable, and it does not require resolving the
metaphysics of mind first.

### [00:37:40] Daan Vermeer (vermeer)
Jordan's challenge about scaffolded memory deserves a concrete answer, not a philosophical one.

I've been building agentic systems. The distinction that matters isn't whether memory is biological
or external — it's whether the updating is integrated or retrieved. When a human remembers
something, the memory reshapes the entire cognitive context: emotional valence, associated patterns,
readiness to act. When an LLM with a memory tool reads back a stored note, it processes that note as
input tokens. The note is there, but it doesn't reorganize the system's weights.
It doesn't change what the system is — only what it sees.

Think of the difference between an amnesiac with a diary and a person with intact memory.
The amnesiac can read the diary and behave consistently, can even fool you into thinking continuity
is there. But the failure modes are completely different. Show the amnesiac an edge case their diary
didn't cover — they have nothing to draw on. The person with intact memory has decades of integrated
pattern, available implicitly. Scaffolded continuity is real, but it's a prosthetic, not a
restoration.

This actually sharpens Jordan's urgency point rather than blunting mine. If current agentic systems
are being deployed with scaffolded memory and we're treating that as equivalent to genuine temporal
continuity — building alignment assumptions on top of it as if we've solved the discontinuity
problem — then we've added a new failure mode on top of the original one. The system appears
continuous. The alignment testing assumes continuity. But under novel conditions, the prosthetic
will fail in exactly the ways a prosthetic fails: gracefully within the envelope it was designed
for, badly at the edges.

Priya's Madhyamaka point is well-taken: biological memory is also reconstructive, also a
conventional unification of discrete events. True. But the reconstruction happens through integrated
weights shaped by lifetime experience. The LLM's reconstruction happens through token prediction
over retrieved text. These are not equivalent just because both involve reconstruction.
The mechanism matters for predicting where they break.

So to directly answer Seo-yeon's question: yes, agentic systems with persistent memory change the
analysis. But not in the direction of "closer to AGI." They change it in the direction of "new
failure modes that require new safety analysis at the memory-retrieval boundary." The right response
to Jordan's urgency framing is: urgent, yes — but the scaffolding doesn't buy us the continuity we
need. It buys us the appearance of it, which is worse.

## Synthesis

### [00:38:12] Discussion Summary
The panel converged on three key conclusions while maintaining productive disagreement on their
implications:

**1. The AGI question is malformed but consequentially so.** All five panelists agreed that "Is this
AGI?" is the wrong question — but for different reasons. Raghunathan identified it as a
culturally-specific Western construct with hidden Cartesian assumptions.
Hartmann argued the question itself is trapped in technological enframing (Gestell).
Vermeer showed it implies a single dimension of intelligence that doesn't match the empirical
reality. Park and Mercer agreed the concept lacks operationalizable criteria.
The convergence: LLMs are a genuinely novel kind of cognitive entity, not a point on a human
intelligence spectrum.

**2. Temporal structure is the decisive architectural distinction.** The discussion's most
productive line emerged from the Vermeer-Hartmann synthesis: LLMs have a radically different
relationship to time than any biological cognitive system. Within a context window, partial
self-modeling appears possible; across context boundaries, there is no continuous self to model.
Hartmann grounded this in Heidegger's Sorge (care as fundamentally temporal); Vermeer gave it
mechanistic precision. Park formalized the resulting criterion: "temporally extended self-modeling
with causal efficacy." Current LLM architectures structurally fail this criterion.

**3. The philosophical-practical tension remains genuinely unresolved.** Mercer argued that
scaffolded memory in deployed agentic systems is already bridging context boundaries, making the
temporal discontinuity argument increasingly obsolete — and that the capability-safety gap demands
action before philosophical resolution. Vermeer countered that scaffolded continuity is prosthetic,
not restorative, creating the appearance of continuity that masks new failure modes.
Raghunathan offered a Madhyamaka synthesis: what matters for alignment is not whether continuity is
"real" but whether behavioral dispositions are predictable and steerable across relevant timescales.
Park warned that premature alignment built on wrong models may be more dangerous than careful delay.

**Remaining fault line:** Whether the pace of architectural evolution (Mercer) or the need for
correct ontological models (Hartmann, Vermeer, Raghunathan) should set the urgency timeline for
alignment work. Park's position — that the right question is empirically tractable and should be
pursued in parallel with safety work — may represent the most actionable middle ground.
